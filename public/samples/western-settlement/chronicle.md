# The Settlement of the American West: A Chronicle, 1803 to 1893

Compiled from standard histories for classroom use. Dates are given as precisely as the record allows.

## Acquisition and exploration

On April 30, 1803, American envoys in Paris signed the Louisiana Purchase treaty, acquiring 828,000 square miles from France for 15 million dollars. The formal transfer of Upper Louisiana took place at St. Louis on March 10, 1804, when the Spanish, French and American flags flew over the town on successive days.

Meriwether Lewis and William Clark's Corps of Discovery left Camp Dubois, across the Mississippi from St. Louis, on May 14, 1804, with about 45 men. They wintered at Fort Mandan near present day Washburn, North Dakota, from November 1804 to April 1805. On November 15, 1805, the expedition reached the Pacific Ocean near the mouth of the Columbia River, and built Fort Clatsop near present day Astoria, Oregon, for the winter of 1805 to 1806. They returned to St. Louis on September 23, 1806.

Zebulon Pike sighted the peak that bears his name near present day Colorado Springs on November 15, 1806, and failed to climb it. John Jacob Astor's Pacific Fur Company founded Fort Astoria at the mouth of the Columbia on April 12, 1811, the first American settlement on the Pacific coast. In October 1812, Robert Stuart's eastbound Astorians crossed South Pass in present day Wyoming, the gentle crossing of the Continental Divide that would carry the wagon trains.

The Adams-Onís Treaty, signed in Washington on February 22, 1819, fixed the boundary with Spanish territory and ceded Florida.

## Trails and the fur trade

William Becknell left Franklin, Missouri, in September 1821 with pack animals and reached Santa Fe on November 16, 1821, opening the Santa Fe Trail to American trade within weeks of Mexican independence. On February 13, 1822, William Ashley advertised in St. Louis for one hundred enterprising young men to ascend the Missouri River, recruiting the mountain men. Jedediah Smith's party rediscovered South Pass in March 1824. The first mountain man rendezvous was held on Henry's Fork of the Green River near present day Burntfork, Wyoming, on July 1, 1825. Smith reached Mission San Gabriel in California on November 27, 1826, the first American to cross overland to California.

Congress passed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830. The forced removal of the Cherokee from Georgia and Tennessee began in May 1838; roughly 16,000 people were marched west to Indian Territory around Tahlequah, Oklahoma, and some 4,000 died on what became known as the Trail of Tears.

Fort William, later Fort Laramie, was established on the Laramie River in Wyoming in June 1834 as a fur trading post. Marcus and Narcissa Whitman founded their mission at Waiilatpu near present day Walla Walla, Washington, in September 1836.

In Texas, the Alamo in San Antonio fell on March 6, 1836, and Sam Houston's army defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto, near present day Houston, on April 21, 1836, securing Texan independence.

## The great migrations

The Bidwell-Bartleson party, the first emigrant wagon train bound for California, left Sapling Grove near Westport, Missouri, in May 1841 and reached the San Joaquin Valley on November 4, 1841. The Great Migration of 1843 departed Elm Grove near Independence, Missouri, on May 22, 1843, with nearly one thousand emigrants and over one hundred wagons, and reached Oregon City in November 1843. Jim Bridger built Fort Bridger in southwestern Wyoming in 1843 to supply the trains. John C. Frémont's second expedition reached the Great Salt Lake in September 1843.

John O'Sullivan coined the phrase Manifest Destiny in a New York magazine article in July 1845. Texas was annexed on December 29, 1845. The Oregon Treaty, signed in Washington on June 15, 1846, set the northern boundary at the 49th parallel. American settlers raised the Bear Flag at Sonoma, California, on June 14, 1846. The Donner Party was trapped by snow at Truckee Lake in the Sierra Nevada in early November 1846; 41 of 87 people died before rescue in the spring of 1847.

Brigham Young's advance party of Latter-day Saints entered the Salt Lake Valley on July 24, 1847, and began laying out a city within days. On November 29, 1847, Cayuse men killed the Whitmans and eleven others at Waiilatpu, triggering the Cayuse War.

## Gold

James Marshall found gold in the tailrace of Sutter's sawmill at Coloma, California, on January 24, 1848. Nine days later, on February 2, 1848, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ended the Mexican-American War and ceded California and the Southwest to the United States. By the end of 1849 some 80,000 forty-niners had reached California, and San Francisco grew from about 1,000 residents in 1848 to roughly 25,000 by 1850. California became a state on September 9, 1850.

The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1851 was negotiated at Horse Creek, Nebraska, on September 17, 1851, with some 10,000 Plains Indians present. The Gadsden Purchase was signed in Mexico City on December 30, 1853. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of May 30, 1854, opened those territories to settlement and to a violent contest over slavery.

Gold strikes on the South Platte set off the Pikes Peak Gold Rush; Denver was founded on November 22, 1858. The Comstock Lode of silver was discovered near Virginia City, Nevada, in June 1859. The Pony Express made its first run from St. Joseph, Missouri, on April 3, 1860, and ran only eighteen months: the transcontinental telegraph was completed at Salt Lake City on October 24, 1861.

## Homesteads and rails

President Lincoln signed the Homestead Act on May 20, 1862, and the Pacific Railway Act on July 1, 1862. Daniel Freeman filed the first homestead claim near Beatrice, Nebraska, shortly after midnight on January 1, 1863. On November 29, 1864, Colorado volunteers under John Chivington killed some 230 Cheyenne and Arapaho, mostly women and children, at Sand Creek in eastern Colorado.

Joseph McCoy shipped the first cattle east from Abilene, Kansas, on September 5, 1867, opening the era of the Chisholm Trail drives. The Fetterman Fight near Fort Phil Kearny, Wyoming, on December 21, 1866, killed 81 soldiers and led to the Fort Laramie Treaty of April 29, 1868, which recognized the Black Hills as Lakota land.

The Central Pacific and Union Pacific met at Promontory Summit, Utah, where the golden spike was driven on May 10, 1869. Two weeks later, on May 24, 1869, John Wesley Powell launched his expedition down the Green and Colorado rivers from Green River, Wyoming. Yellowstone became the first national park on March 1, 1872. Joseph Glidden of DeKalb, Illinois, received his patent for barbed wire on November 24, 1874, which did more to close the open range than any treaty.

## The Indian wars and the closing frontier

George Custer's expedition confirmed gold in the Black Hills in August 1874; Deadwood, South Dakota, sprang up in the spring of 1876. On June 25, 1876, Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho warriors destroyed Custer's battalion at the Little Bighorn in Montana. Chief Joseph surrendered the Nez Perce in the Bear Paw Mountains of Montana on October 5, 1877, forty miles from the Canadian border.

The gunfight near the O.K. Corral in Tombstone, Arizona, took place on October 26, 1881. The Northern Pacific Railroad was completed at Gold Creek, Montana, on September 8, 1883. Geronimo surrendered at Skeleton Canyon, Arizona, on September 4, 1886. The winter of 1886 to 1887, the Big Die-Up, killed as much as 90 percent of some herds on the northern plains around Miles City, Montana, in January 1887, ending the open range cattle boom. The Dawes Act of February 8, 1887, broke reservations into allotments.

At noon on April 22, 1889, some 50,000 people rushed into the Unassigned Lands of Oklahoma; Guthrie and Oklahoma City had populations of 10,000 by nightfall. North Dakota, South Dakota, Montana and Washington entered the Union in November 1889. On December 29, 1890, soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry killed between 250 and 300 Lakota at Wounded Knee Creek, South Dakota.

The Census Bureau in Washington reported in 1890 that a frontier line could no longer be traced. Frederick Jackson Turner read his essay on the significance of the frontier to the American Historical Association in Chicago on July 12, 1893, during the World's Columbian Exposition.
